The question

I wanted to forecast next-day aggregate trading volume across S&P 500 constituents using only information available by the previous close. The natural benchmark is simple but difficult to beat: trading volume is persistent, so today's level already contains substantial information about tomorrow's.

Raw share volume, however, is a difficult target over a long sample. Index membership changes, stock splits mechanically alter share counts, and the overall level of market activity shifts considerably through time. A model fitted directly to that level can therefore spend much of its capacity learning long-run changes rather than forecasting tomorrow.

The project therefore has two parts: first construct a more stable forecasting target, then test whether a flexible nonlinear model adds meaningfully to a compact statistical time-series model.

Setup

Rather than forecast raw volume directly, I modelled log volume relative to its trailing one-year level:

\[ y_t = \log V_t - \frac{1}{252}\sum_{i=1}^{252} \log V_{t-i}. \]

The transformation is strictly backward-looking. Taking logs stabilises the scale, while subtracting the previous 252-day mean of log volume removes much of the slow movement in the market-wide level. The remaining series is centred around zero and leaves shorter-run variation for the models to forecast.

The transformation also changes the series' autocorrelation mechanically. I therefore treat persistence in the transformed target as useful for forecasting, but not as evidence that raw trading volume necessarily follows a structural long-memory process.

Approach

Pre-holdout analysis suggested three useful sources of information: the target's own recent history, recurring calendar effects, and the recent market-volatility state, including lagged VIX and absolute market returns.

I then compared two deliberately different forecasting models using these same broad information sources.

ARFIMAX-GARCH

A compact statistical time-series model. Fractional differencing captures persistent dependence in the transformed target, autoregressive and external predictors capture shorter-run effects, and GARCH allows residual volatility to change through time. The specification and feature set were kept deliberately small.

XGBoost

A nonlinear tree-based alternative. XGBoost can learn thresholds and interactions automatically: it can allow the effect of recent volume to depend on the current volatility state, for instance. I compared a lean specification with a larger version containing additional volatility lags and rolling statistics.

Validation design

Model development used six expanding-window validation blocks from 2011–2016. At each step, the model was fitted only on earlier observations and then produced one-step-ahead forecasts as new lagged information became available.

The initial feature families were informed by exploratory analysis of the broader pre-2017 sample, so I treat these folds as development evidence rather than an untouched estimate of generalisation. The cleaner test is the 2017–2018 holdout, which was left unused during model selection and scored once.

All models were ultimately compared in the original units of billions of shares and against the same persistence benchmark:

\[ \widehat V_{t+1}=V_t. \]

Results

Both models beat persistence by a wide margin. XGBoost performed best, but only narrowly.

On the final holdout, XGBoost achieved an RMSE of 0.357 billion shares, compared with 0.367 for ARFIMAX-GARCH and 0.608 for simply carrying today's volume forward. In MSE terms, the two models reduced persistence error by 65.6% and 63.5%, respectively.

The similarity is also visible during development, where the two models' MSEs differed by only 0.0006 bn². With only two holdout years and serially dependent daily observations, I do not interpret XGBoost's small lead as evidence that nonlinear models are generally superior. The stronger result is that two very different modelling approaches extracted almost the same predictive structure from the data.

Additional complexity did not help. Expanding XGBoost's feature set worsened development MSE from 0.2406 to 0.2456, while an explicit volatility interaction did not improve the best parametric specification. The evidence therefore favours a compact set of predictors built around recent volume, calendar effects and market volatility.

What was difficult

The first challenge was separating useful target engineering from structural interpretation. The trailing normalisation makes volume easier to model, but it also changes the dependence structure of the series. Persistence after the transformation is therefore useful for forecasting without necessarily telling us that raw trading volume itself has long memory.

The second issue appears when forecasts are converted back from log space into shares. That conversion requires a variance correction: ARFIMAX-GARCH supplies a time-varying conditional variance, while XGBoost uses a constant residual-variance adjustment. Both are approximations because the forecast errors are asymmetric and heavy-tailed. Together with the models' tendency to miss the largest volume spikes, this means I view them as strong conditional-mean forecasters, not complete models of the distribution of future volume.

Limits and what's next

The final 2017–2018 holdout was calmer than much of the development period, so its absolute error levels may be favourable. Raw share volume is also sensitive to stock splits and changing index composition. A useful robustness check would repeat the exercise using dollar volume or turnover relative to shares outstanding.

The more interesting extension is distributional forecasting. Both models struggle most on extreme-volume days, so quantile or probabilistic forecasts could estimate the likelihood and size of those events directly rather than treating them simply as large point-forecast errors.
