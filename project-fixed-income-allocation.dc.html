Overview

We worked with Wellington Management's fixed-income team to build a systematic input for a discretionary PM's monthly sector-allocation decisions.

We forecast next-month excess returns across seven global bond sectors using information on carry and valuation, recent market behaviour and the broader market environment. Rather than construct a standalone trading portfolio, the forecasts were converted into constrained over- and underweights around the Bloomberg Global Aggregate.

Over roughly five out-of-sample years, the resulting portfolio achieved a 1.29 net information ratio, generated 6.4 bp of average monthly active return, and outperformed the benchmark in 73% of months. The forecasts also produced an average monthly Rank IC of 0.32. Diagnostics suggested that the returns were driven more by persistent carry and relative-value exposures than by reliable month-to-month credit timing.

The allocation problem

This project was built with Wellington Management's fixed-income team to support a real monthly portfolio decision: where should a discretionary PM run sector overweights and underweights relative to the benchmark?

The PM already had an existing portfolio, benchmark exposures and fundamental and macro views. Our role was therefore not to construct an autonomous bond portfolio, but to add a systematic cross-sector signal to that process: a quantitative view of relative opportunities that could help inform the team's allocation decisions.

We worked across seven global bond sectors spanning government debt, investment-grade credit, high yield, emerging-market credit and municipals. Using monthly data from 2015–2025, the model forecast each sector's next-month excess return. Those forecasts were then translated into constrained tilts around the Bloomberg Global Aggregate.

Signals & model

For each sector \(s\), we used a regularised linear model to forecast next-month excess return:

\[ \widehat{ER}_{s,t+1} = \alpha_s+\boldsymbol{\beta}_s^\top\mathbf{x}_{s,t}, \]

where \(\mathbf{x}_{s,t}\) contains only information available at the end of month \(t\).

The candidate predictors were organised into three economic groups. Carry and valuation described the income and relative pricing of each sector. Recent market behaviour captured trailing performance and changes in credit spreads. Market conditions added broader information from volatility, financial conditions, the yield curve, equities, currencies and rates.

The monthly sample was relatively short, so variable selection was deliberately conservative. We started from economically motivated predictors, used Rank IC to assess their predictive ordering, and used Spearman correlations and variance-inflation diagnostics to identify redundant inputs. Regularisation was then tuned through expanding-window time-series validation, with weak predictors shrunk rather than allowed to fit freely.

From forecasts to benchmark tilts

The forecasts only become useful once they translate into a portfolio decision. Each month, we compared the seven predicted excess returns cross-sectionally, converted them into clipped standardised scores and used those scores to tilt the benchmark weights:

\[ w_{s,t} = w^{BM}_{s,t}+\Delta w_{s,t}, \qquad \sum_s \Delta w_{s,t}=0, \qquad \Delta w_{s,t} \propto \widetilde z\!\left(\widehat{ER}_{s,t+1}\right), \]

where \(\widetilde z\) is the clipped cross-sectional z-score.

Sectors with stronger relative forecasts received overweights, funded by underweights in sectors with weaker forecasts. Because the active tilts sum to zero, the model reallocates weight around the benchmark rather than choosing a new portfolio from scratch.

The portfolio remained long-only and fully invested, subject to concentration and duration constraints. Performance was then evaluated against the Bloomberg Global Aggregate after assumed trading costs.

The forecasts were useful out of sample both statistically and economically. Their average monthly Rank IC, the Spearman correlation between predicted and realised sector rankings, was 0.32, and it was positive in 70% of months. After translating those forecasts into benchmark tilts, the portfolio generated 6.4 bp of average monthly gross active return, outperformed the benchmark in 73% of months, and achieved a 1.29 net information ratio.

Turnover was only about 1% per month, so assumed trading costs had little effect on the headline result: the information ratio moved from 1.30 gross to 1.29 net.

What the model appeared to capture

The allocation paths were persistent rather than rapidly changing, and the model tended to favour higher-income credit sectors. Performance also weakened when credit spreads widened sharply. Together, those patterns are more consistent with a persistent carry or relative-value exposure than with reliable month-to-month spread timing.

That distinction matters for how the model should be used. We viewed it as a systematic input into relative sector allocation, one signal alongside a discretionary portfolio manager's fundamental and macro views, rather than as a standalone credit-timing model.
